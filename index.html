Part 1: Why Surveys Matter 📊

“Ask the wrong people the wrong questions…”

…and you get wrong answers!

💡 Key Insight

Surveys are a primary data collection method. Good survey design = good data = reliable insights!

What Makes a Good Survey?

Three Critical Components:

1. Right Sample

Who you ask

  • Representative of population
  • Sufficient size
  • Unbiased selection

2. Right Questions

What you ask

  • Clear and unbiased
  • Appropriate response options
  • Logical flow

3. Right Method

How you ask

  • Appropriate channel
  • Good timing
  • Minimize bias

⚠️ Weak in any area?

Your results will be unreliable, no matter how sophisticated your analysis!

Part 2: Sampling Strategies 🎯

Sampling = Selecting a subset of a population to study.

Why sample?

  • ✅ Cost-effective: Can’t survey everyone (time, money)
  • ✅ Faster: Get results quicker than a census
  • ✅ Practical: Some populations are huge or hard to reach
  • ✅ Statistical validity: A good sample can represent the whole population

📋 Key terms

Population = entire group you want to understand
Sample = subset you actually study
Sampling frame = list or source from which you select

Sampling: Key Concepts

Sample Size (n):

  • Larger sample → more precise estimates
  • But diminishing returns (1000 → 2000 less gain than 100 → 200)
  • Depends on population variability and desired precision

Representativeness:

  • Sample should reflect the population characteristics
  • Age, gender, region, behavior patterns, etc.
  • Non-representative sample → biased results

💡 Remember

Big sample ≠ good sample. A biased sample of 10,000 is worse than a representative sample of 500!

Two Main Sampling Approaches

Probability Sampling

Every member has a known, non-zero chance of selection

Advantages:

  • Can calculate sampling error
  • Generalizable to population
  • Scientifically rigorous

Disadvantages:

  • Requires sampling frame
  • More expensive/time-consuming
  • May be impractical

Non-Probability Sampling

Selection based on convenience, judgment, or availability

Advantages:

  • Faster and cheaper
  • Easier to implement
  • Good for exploratory research

Disadvantages:

  • Cannot generalize statistically
  • Unknown bias
  • No sampling error calculation

Probability Sampling Methods

1. Simple Random Sampling

Every member has equal chance of selection (like drawing names from a hat).

How: Number everyone, use random number generator to select.

Pros: Unbiased, simple concept
Cons: Need complete list; may miss subgroups

Example: Select 200 students from 2,000 by random number

🎯 Best for

Homogeneous populations where you have a complete list!

Probability Sampling Methods (cont.)

2. Systematic Sampling

Select every k-th member from a list.

How: Choose starting point randomly, then every 10th, 20th, etc.

Pros: Simple, spreads across list
Cons: If list has pattern, may introduce bias

Example: Every 5th customer entering a store

⚠️ Watch out

If list is ordered (e.g., by department), systematic sampling might skip certain groups!

Probability Sampling Methods (cont.)

3. Stratified Sampling

Divide population into strata (subgroups), then sample from each stratum.

How:

  1. Divide by key characteristic (age, region, etc.)
  2. Sample randomly within each stratum
  3. Can use proportional or equal allocation

Pros: Ensures representation of subgroups, more precise
Cons: Need to know strata in advance

Example: Survey 100 students: 40 Year 1, 30 Year 2, 30 Year 3 (proportional to enrollment)

🎯 Best for

When subgroups are important and you want to ensure all are represented!

Probability Sampling Methods (cont.)

4. Cluster Sampling

Divide population into clusters (groups), randomly select clusters, survey all in selected clusters.

How:

  1. Divide into natural groups (schools, villages, etc.)
  2. Randomly select clusters
  3. Survey everyone in selected clusters (or sub-sample within)

Pros: Cost-effective for geographically spread populations
Cons: Higher sampling error if clusters are very different

Example: Select 10 schools from 100, survey all students in those 10

📋 Difference from stratified

Stratified = sample from all strata. Cluster = sample entire clusters, not all clusters!

Stratified vs Cluster: Visual Comparison

Aspect Stratified Cluster
Division By key characteristic (age, gender) By natural groups (location, class)
Sampling Sample from every stratum Sample some clusters only
Within group Random selection Usually all members (or sub-sample)
Goal Ensure subgroup representation Reduce cost/logistics
Precision Usually higher Usually lower

💡 Rule of thumb

Stratified when subgroups matter. Cluster when logistics/cost matter!

Non-Probability Sampling Methods

1. Convenience Sampling

Select whoever is easiest to reach.

How: Survey people in the cafeteria, on the street, your friends

Pros: Fast, cheap
Cons: High bias, not representative

Example: Surveying classmates about university policy

⚠️ Caution

Good for quick feedback, but cannot generalize to population!

Non-Probability Sampling Methods (cont.)

2. Purposive (Judgmental) Sampling

Researcher selects participants based on judgment of who is “typical” or “informative.”

How: Choose experts, key informants, or specific characteristics

Pros: Good for qualitative, exploratory research
Cons: Subjective, bias from researcher’s judgment

Example: Interview 10 experienced teachers about curriculum

🎯 Best for

Qualitative research, expert opinions, case studies!

Non-Probability Sampling Methods (cont.)

3. Quota Sampling

Like stratified, but selection within quotas is non-random.

How: Set quotas (e.g., 50 males, 50 females), fill by convenience

Pros: Ensures subgroup representation, faster than stratified
Cons: Bias within quotas, no random selection

Example: Survey 100 people: 50 male, 50 female, stopping when quota is filled

📋 Difference from stratified

Stratified = random within strata. Quota = convenience within quotas!

Non-Probability Sampling Methods (cont.)

4. Snowball Sampling

Participants refer others (like a snowball growing).

How: Start with a few, ask them to refer others, and so on

Pros: Good for hard-to-reach populations
Cons: Bias (network effects), not representative

Example: Surveying people with a rare condition through support groups

🎯 Best for

Hidden or hard-to-reach populations (rare diseases, illegal activities, marginalized groups)!

Choosing a Sampling Method

Situation Good choice
Need to generalize statistically Probability (simple random, stratified)
Subgroups must be represented Stratified
Large geographic area, budget limited Cluster
Quick exploratory feedback Convenience
Expert opinions needed Purposive
Hard-to-reach population Snowball
Ensure quotas, faster than stratified Quota

🎯 Best practice

Use probability for conclusions. Use non-probability for exploration, then validate with probability!

Sample Size Considerations

How many people should you survey?

Factors:

  • Population size (for small populations, you need higher %)
  • Desired precision (margin of error)
  • Confidence level (usually 95%)
  • Population variability (more diverse → larger sample)
  • Budget and time

Rule of thumb: For simple random sampling, n = 400 gives ±5% margin of error at 95% confidence (large population).

📋 Online calculators

Use sample size calculators (e.g., SurveyMonkey, Qualtrics) to determine n for your needs!

Part 3: Understanding Bias 🎭

Bias = Systematic error that skews results in one direction.

Why it matters:

  • Bias makes your sample unrepresentative
  • Leads to wrong conclusions
  • Cannot be fixed by increasing sample size

💡 Key point

Random error averages out with larger sample. Bias does not!

Types of Bias in Surveys

We’ll cover:

  1. Sampling Bias (who you select)
  2. Response Bias (who responds)
  3. Question Wording Bias (how you ask)
  4. Interviewer Bias (who asks)
  5. Social Desirability Bias (what people admit)

⚠️ Multiple sources

Bias can enter at every stage of survey process!

1. Sampling Bias

Definition: Some members of the population have zero or lower chance of being selected.

Common causes:

  • Using a biased sampling frame (e.g., phone book misses people without landlines)
  • Convenience sampling (only easy-to-reach people)
  • Voluntary sampling (only motivated people)

Example: Surveying only students in the library about study habits → misses students who study elsewhere!

🎯 How to avoid

Use probability sampling with a complete, unbiased sampling frame!

2. Non-Response Bias

Definition: Those who respond differ systematically from those who don’t respond.

Common causes:

  • Low response rate
  • Survey too long or boring
  • Topic not interesting to some groups
  • Timing (e.g., weekday survey misses night-shift workers)

Example: Customer satisfaction survey → happy customers respond, unhappy ones ignore → inflated satisfaction scores!

🎯 How to reduce

  • Keep surveys short
  • Offer incentives
  • Follow up with reminders
  • Make it easy (mobile-friendly, multiple languages)

3. Question Wording Bias (Leading Questions)

Definition: Question wording pushes respondents toward a certain answer.

Examples:

  • ❌ “Don’t you agree that our excellent customer service is the best?”

  • ✅ “How would you rate our customer service?” (1–5 scale)

  • ❌ “How much do you love our new product?”

  • ✅ “What is your opinion of our new product?”

⚠️ Watch out

Loaded words (“excellent,” “love”), double negatives, or assumptions!

4. Interviewer Bias

Definition: Interviewer’s presence, appearance, or behavior influences responses.

Common causes:

  • Interviewer’s tone, body language
  • Demographic mismatch (e.g., young interviewer, elderly respondent)
  • Interviewer expectations (unintentionally guiding answers)

Example: Face-to-face survey about sensitive topics (income, health) → respondent gives “socially acceptable” answer to please interviewer.

🎯 How to reduce

  • Train interviewers to be neutral
  • Use self-administered surveys (online, paper) for sensitive topics
  • Standardize interview scripts

5. Social Desirability Bias

Definition: Respondents give answers that make them look good or socially acceptable, not their true opinion.

Examples:

  • Over-report voting, exercise, charitable giving
  • Under-report smoking, alcohol, illegal behavior

How to reduce:

  • Ensure anonymity and confidentiality
  • Use indirect questions (e.g., “How many of your friends smoke?” rather than “Do you smoke?”)
  • Randomized response technique for very sensitive topics

📋 Especially problematic

For health, behavior, income, and political topics!

Other Common Biases

Bias What it is Example
Recall Bias People forget or misremember “How many times did you eat fast food last month?”
Acquiescence Bias Tendency to agree (yes-saying) “Do you agree?” questions all in one direction
Order Bias First or last options chosen more List of brands; first brand gets more picks
Extreme Response Bias Always pick extreme (1 or 5) Some cultures/people avoid middle

🎯 How to reduce

Recall → ask about recent/specific events. Acquiescence → balance positive/negative. Order → randomize!

Avoiding Bias: Summary

Stage Bias risk How to avoid
Sampling Sampling bias Probability sampling, good frame
Contact Non-response bias High response rate, incentives
Question design Wording bias Neutral, clear language
Administration Interviewer bias Train interviewers, use self-admin
Response Social desirability Anonymity, indirect questions

💡 Golden rule

Test your survey with a small pilot group before full launch!

Part 4: Survey Design Principles 📝

Good survey design = clear, unbiased, respondent-friendly

📋 Goals

  1. Get accurate data (minimize bias)
  2. Get complete data (high response rate)
  3. Make it easy for respondents

Survey Structure: Best Practices

Typical flow:

  1. Introduction
    • Purpose, who you are, how data will be used
    • Confidentiality/anonymity assurance
    • Estimated time
  2. Screening questions (if needed)
    • Ensure respondent is in target group
  3. Main questions
    • Start easy, engaging (warm-up)
    • Sensitive or demographic questions at the end
    • Logical grouping (topic by topic)
  4. Closing
    • Thank you
    • Contact info for questions
    • Incentive info (if any)

🎯 First impression matters

Make intro clear and friendly; ask easy questions first!

Question Order & Flow

General principles:

  • Easy → Hard: Start with simple, non-threatening questions
  • General → Specific: Broad questions first, narrow later
  • Sensitive last: Demographics, income, personal info at the end (once rapport is built)
  • Logical grouping: Keep related questions together (all about product A, then product B)

Example flow:

  1. “How often do you shop online?” (easy, general)
  2. “Which online stores do you use?” (specific)
  3. “What is your annual income?” (sensitive, at end)

⚠️ Avoid fatigue

Don’t put all hard/long questions in a row; break up with easier ones!

Survey Length

Optimal length depends on:

  • Channel: Online (5–10 min), phone (10–15 min), face-to-face (longer ok)
  • Audience: Engaged audience can go longer
  • Incentive: Offering reward → people tolerate longer

Rule of thumb:

  • ✅ 10 minutes or less for general online surveys
  • ✅ 15–20 questions max (if mixed types)
  • ❌ Avoid 50+ questions unless essential

💡 Test it

Take the survey yourself and time it. If you’re bored, so will respondents be!

Part 5: Question Types & Best Practices 🎯

Question types:

  1. Closed-ended (fixed response options)
  2. Open-ended (free text)
  3. Rating scales (Likert, semantic differential, etc.)

📋 Each type has pros/cons and best use cases!

Closed-Ended Questions

Definition: Respondent selects from predefined options.

Types:

  • Dichotomous: Yes/No, True/False
  • Multiple choice: Select one from list
  • Multiple response: Select all that apply
  • Ranking: Order items by preference

Pros: Easy to answer, easy to analyze, comparable
Cons: May miss nuance, force respondent into a box

Example: “Which platform do you use most? (a) Facebook (b) Instagram (c) Twitter (d) TikTok”

🎯 Best for

Quantitative data, large samples, standardized analysis!

Open-Ended Questions

Definition: Respondent answers in their own words (free text).

Pros: Rich detail, unexpected insights, no forced choice
Cons: Time-consuming to answer and analyze, harder to compare

Example: “What do you like most about our product?”

When to use:

  • Exploratory research
  • “Why?” questions
  • Feedback and suggestions
  • When you don’t know all possible answers

⚠️ Use sparingly

Too many open-ended questions → low completion rate!

Rating Scales: Likert Scale

Likert Scale: Agree/disagree on a 5- or 7-point scale.

Example:

“Our customer service is excellent.”

1 = Strongly disagree | 2 = Disagree | 3 = Neutral | 4 = Agree | 5 = Strongly agree

Pros: Easy to answer, ordinal data for analysis
Cons: Neutral option can be a “cop-out”; scale interpretation varies

Tips:

  • Use odd (5, 7) for neutral option, or even (4, 6) to force a lean
  • Keep same scale throughout (don’t mix 5-point and 7-point)

📋 Most common

Widely used in satisfaction, opinion, and attitude surveys!

Rating Scales: Other Types

Semantic Differential:

“How would you rate our product?”
Boring 1 — 2 — 3 — 4 — 5 Exciting

Numeric Rating:

“On a scale of 0–10, how likely are you to recommend us?”

Frequency:

“How often do you exercise?”
Never | Rarely | Sometimes | Often | Always

🎯 Match scale to question

Use frequency scale for “how often,” semantic for “how you feel,” numeric for NPS/recommendations!

Writing Good Questions: Dos and Don’ts

✅ Do:

  • Use simple, clear language (avoid jargon)
  • Ask one thing at a time (no double-barreled questions)
  • Be specific (“last month” vs “recently”)
  • Use neutral wording (avoid leading)
  • Provide balanced options (if Agree/Disagree, include both)

❌ Don’t:

  • Use double negatives (“Don’t you disagree…?”)
  • Ask two questions in one (“Do you like our price and quality?”)
  • Use technical terms unless audience is expert
  • Assume (“Given that our product is the best…”)
  • Make it too long or too vague

Examples: Good vs Bad Questions

Bad ❌ Good ✅
“Don’t you think our amazing service is great?” “How would you rate our service?” (1–5)
“How satisfied are you with our price and quality?” “How satisfied are you with our price?” (separate question for quality)
“How often do you eat healthy food?” “How many servings of fruits and vegetables did you eat yesterday?”
“What is your approximate annual income in the general vicinity of last year?” “What is your annual household income?” (provide ranges)

💡 Clarity is king

If a question confuses you, it will confuse respondents!

Response Options: Best Practices

Mutually exclusive: Options should not overlap.

  • ❌ Age: 18–25, 25–35, 35–45 (25 and 35 appear twice!)
  • ✅ Age: 18–24, 25–34, 35–44

Collectively exhaustive: Cover all possible answers.

  • Add “Other” or “None” if needed
  • Example: “Which device do you use? (a) Desktop (b) Laptop (c) Tablet (d) Phone (e) Other”

Balanced: For agree/disagree, provide equal positive and negative options.

🎯 Test options

Pilot survey will reveal if options are missing or overlap!

Demographic Questions

Purpose: Segment responses by age, gender, income, location, etc.

Best practices:

  • Place at the end (unless screening)
  • Explain why you need it
  • Offer “Prefer not to answer” option for sensitive items
  • Use ranges (age: 18–24, income: $20k–$40k) not exact numbers

Example:

“To better understand our audience, we’d like to know a bit about you. This information is confidential.”

  • Gender: Male / Female / Other / Prefer not to say
  • Age: 18–24 / 25–34 / 35–44 / 45–54 / 55+

📋 Only ask what you’ll use

Don’t collect data you won’t analyze!

Survey Channels & Methods

Channel Pros Cons Best for
Online (web, email) Fast, cheap, wide reach Needs internet, may miss offline populations Tech-savvy, large samples
Phone Personal, can clarify Expensive, time-consuming, declining response rates Older audiences, complex topics
Face-to-face Highest response, rich data Very expensive, time-consuming, interviewer bias In-depth, sensitive topics
Paper (mail, in-person) No tech needed Slow, data entry needed, low response rate Offline populations, older audiences
Mobile app/SMS Convenient, immediate Short surveys only, limited question types Quick feedback, location-based

🎯 Mix channels if needed

E.g., online for most, phone follow-up for non-responders!

Pilot Testing Your Survey

Before launching to full sample, test with a small group (10–30 people):

Check for:

  • Clarity: Do questions make sense?
  • Flow: Is order logical? Any fatigue?
  • Technical issues: Survey platform works?
  • Timing: How long does it take?
  • Response patterns: Any questions everyone skips or answers the same?

Revise based on feedback, then launch!

💡 Don’t skip this step

Pilot testing catches problems before they ruin your data!

Ethics in Survey Research

Key principles:

  • Informed consent: Respondents know what they’re participating in
  • Confidentiality/Anonymity: Protect respondent identity and data
  • No harm: Don’t ask questions that distress or endanger
  • Voluntary: Respondents can opt out or skip questions
  • Honest reporting: Report all results, not just what you like

Example: “Your responses are anonymous and will only be used for research purposes.”

⚠️ Check your institution

Many universities require ethics approval (IRB) for surveys involving human subjects!

Survey Design Checklist ✅

Before you launch:

  • ☑️ Clear objective: What question am I answering?
  • ☑️ Right sample: Probability method if generalizing; adequate size
  • ☑️ Unbiased questions: Neutral wording, no leading
  • ☑️ Logical flow: Easy → hard, general → specific, sensitive at end
  • ☑️ Appropriate length: <10 min for online, <20 questions
  • ☑️ Balanced options: Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive
  • ☑️ Pilot tested: Small group feedback
  • ☑️ Ethics: Consent, confidentiality, voluntary

🎯 Quality over quantity

Better to have 300 good responses than 3,000 biased ones!

Practice Exercise 1 🏋️

Identify the sampling method:

  1. A researcher divides a company’s employees by department, then randomly selects 20% from each department.
  2. A pollster stands outside a shopping mall and surveys the first 100 people who agree to participate.
  3. A health study starts with 10 patients with a rare disease and asks them to refer others with the same condition.
  4. A school randomly selects 5 out of 20 classrooms and surveys all students in those 5 classrooms.

Sample Answer: Exercise 1

  1. Stratified sampling (divide by department, sample from each)
  2. Convenience sampling (whoever is available and agrees)
  3. Snowball sampling (referrals from initial participants)
  4. Cluster sampling (randomly select classrooms, survey all in selected classrooms)

💡 Notice

1 and 4 are probability methods; 2 and 3 are non-probability!

Sample Answer: Exercise 2

  2. Question wording bias / leading question (pushes toward “agree”)

  4. Interviewer bias + social desirability bias (respondent may under-report to please interviewer)

💡 Real surveys often have multiple biases

Always evaluate at every stage!

Key Takeaways 🎯

Sampling Strategies

  • Probability (random, stratified, cluster, systematic) → generalizable, rigorous
  • Non-probability (convenience, purposive, quota, snowball) → faster, exploratory
  • Choose based on goals, budget, population

Bias

  • Sampling bias (wrong people selected)
  • Non-response bias (responders differ from non-responders)
  • Question wording bias (leading questions)
  • Interviewer & social desirability bias (respondent doesn’t answer honestly)
  • Reduce bias: probability sampling, neutral questions, anonymity, pilot test

Survey Design

  • Structure: Intro → easy questions → main → sensitive/demographics → close
  • Questions: Clear, one topic, neutral, specific
  • Options: Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, balanced
  • Length: <10 min online, <20 questions
  • Pilot test before launch!

Practical Tips for Your Survey Project 📋

Starting your survey:

  1. Define your goal: What do you want to learn?
  2. Identify population: Who should you survey?
  3. Choose sampling method: Probability if generalizing, non-probability if exploratory
  4. Draft questions: Keep it short, clear, unbiased
  5. Design flow: Intro → warm-up → main → demographics → close
  6. Select platform: Google Forms, SurveyMonkey, Qualtrics, etc.
  7. Pilot test: 10–20 people, revise based on feedback
  8. Launch: Distribute, remind, collect
  9. Analyze: Clean data, check for bias, analyze by demographics
  10. Report: Share findings, acknowledge limitations

🎯 Remember

A good survey takes planning. Don’t rush it!
